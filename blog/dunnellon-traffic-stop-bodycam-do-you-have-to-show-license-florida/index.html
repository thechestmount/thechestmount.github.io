Dunnellon Traffic Stop Bodycam: Do You Have to Show Your License in Florida?

Nov 20, 20267 min readBy The Chest Mount

Marion County deputies broke a car window and used a Taser after a Dunnellon driver refused to give her license during a speeding stop, MCSO says. What Florida law says about showing your license and registration, and the resisting charges. Not legal advice.

Share Share on X Facebook Reddit

A speeding stop in Dunnellon, Florida, is one of the most-shared bodycam videos of the week. The Marion County Sheriff's Office (MCSO) released the footage itself, and stations including WCJB, WFLA and NewsNation picked it up. It shows a driver talking to a deputy through a closed window, refusing to hand over her license and registration, and a stop that, according to the deputy, might have ended in a warning ending with a broken window, a Taser and an arrest instead.

It also raises a question a lot of drivers search: do you have to show your license at a traffic stop in Florida? This explainer covers what the sheriff's office and news outlets have reported, then walks through what Florida law actually says. It's general information, not legal advice. The driver has only been charged. She is presumed innocent unless proven guilty in court.

What happened in Dunnellon, according to MCSO

The account below comes from the sheriff's office, as reported by WCJB, WFLA (republished by NewsNation) and the Toronto Sun. MCSO shared the video in a post on its official Facebook page. We're linking to those reports rather than hosting or embedding the footage.

  • The stop. On Nov. 11, 2026, a deputy stopped a car on North Williams Street in Dunnellon after measuring its speed at 52 mph in a 35 mph zone, according to MCSO.
  • The closed window. NewsNation reports the deputy asked the driver to roll her window down, and she kept talking to him with it rolled up. The deputy asked for her license and registration. MCSO identified the driver as Dawn Collier, 66, and said she repeatedly refused to provide them.
  • The offer of a warning. On the video, as quoted by NewsNation, the deputy says: "Yes ma'am you can either roll your window down and we can have a very nice, civil conversation. I might even just write you a warning or we can go this a completely separate different way." He's also heard saying, "You're obstructing an investigation," and later that if she didn't roll the window down, "I call my supervisor, we break your window, we pull you out of this vehicle and then I take you to the jail."
  • The supervisor. The driver asked for a supervisor, and the deputy called one to the scene. According to NewsNation, the supervisor asked her to identify herself. When she refused, he said, "Take her out."
  • The arrest. Deputies broke the driver's side window and pulled her out of the car. MCSO says she punched and kicked at deputies before she was stunned with a Taser. She was taken to the Marion County Jail.
  • The charges. The sheriff's office said she was charged with two counts of battery on a law enforcement officer, resisting an officer with violence and resisting an officer without violence (obstruction). In its post, as quoted by the Toronto Sun, MCSO wrote: "What could've ended with a warning ended with three felony charges and one misdemeanor charge."

As of Nov. 20, 2026, we haven't found any public statement from the driver or a lawyer for her, or any court outcome. No serious injuries have been reported.

What the footage can and can't tell you

This is agency-released video, and the agency chose what to post. A bodycam shows what one camera saw from one angle. It can't show everything both sides said before the camera started, what the driver understood or believed, or how a judge will view the moment deputies decided to break the window. Those are questions for the court. Do you have to show your license at a traffic stop in Florida?

Yes. Section 322.15(1) of the Florida Statutes says every licensed driver must have the license "in his or her immediate possession at all times when operating a motor vehicle" and "shall present or submit the same upon the demand of a law enforcement officer." A few details matter:

  • Digital licenses count, with a catch. You can present a digital license instead of the card. But if the officer can't immediately verify it, the law says you must present the printed license on demand.
  • Registration too. Section 320.0605 says the registration certificate (or an allowed copy) must be in the vehicle or with the driver and "shall be exhibited upon demand of any authorized law enforcement officer."
  • The infraction itself is noncriminal. Under 322.15(4), failing to display a license is a noncriminal traffic infraction, punished as a nonmoving violation. Under 322.15(2), the officer must have you put your fingerprints on any citation or collect them electronically.

So the refusal alone is a ticket-level issue on paper. The bigger risks come from what can follow it.

Can refusing turn into a crime?

It can, depending on the facts. Here are the statutes that come up most often:

  • Refusing to sign a citation. For criminal traffic violations and violations that require a mandatory court hearing, section 318.14(2)-(3) requires the driver to sign and accept the citation, and willfully refusing is a second-degree misdemeanor.
  • Resisting without violence. Section 843.02 makes it a first-degree misdemeanor to resist, obstruct or oppose an officer "in the lawful execution of any legal duty" without violence. That's up to one year in jail and a $1,000 fine. This is the "obstruction" count MCSO listed. Because the duty must be lawful, the legality of the stop and of each order can become part of the defense.
  • Resisting with violence. Section 843.01 makes it a third-degree felony to knowingly and willfully resist an officer "by offering or doing violence" to the officer.
  • Battery on a law enforcement officer. Florida's battery statute, 784.03, covers intentionally touching or striking someone against their will. Section 784.07 reclassifies a battery knowingly committed on an officer performing official duties from a first-degree misdemeanor to a third-degree felony.

Under sections 775.082 and 775.083, a third-degree felony carries a maximum of five years in prison and a $5,000 fine. Prosecutors have to prove every element of each charge, including, for the battery and violent-resisting counts, that the conduct was knowing and willful. MCSO's account is one side of that.

Do you have to roll down your window for police in Florida?

We didn't find a Florida statute that says "roll down your window" in those words. But the law does require you to present your license and registration on demand, and in practice that usually means opening the window far enough to hand them over and talk with the officer. Officers can also order you out of the car. In Pennsylvania v. Mimms (1977), the U.S. Supreme Court held that officers may order a driver out of a vehicle during a lawful traffic stop.

What a stop is not is a felony on its own. Most Florida speeding tickets are noncriminal infractions handled under chapter 318, and as the deputy suggested on the video, an officer can choose to give a warning. Our warning vs. ticket explainer covers how that discretion works in Florida.

The safer way to handle a stop you disagree with

If you think a stop is wrong, the side of the road usually isn't where that gets settled. Common know-your-rights guidance, such as the ACLU's advice for drivers who are stopped, comes down to the same steps: stay calm, keep your hands visible, provide the documents the law requires, and say clearly if you don't consent to a search or want to stay silent. Then challenge the stop or the ticket later in traffic court, or file a complaint with the agency. Asking for a supervisor is allowed, but it doesn't pause your legal obligations while you wait. How to request the records in Florida

Florida's public records law, chapter 119, is one of the broadest in the country, but records tied to an active criminal case can be withheld or redacted while it's open. To ask for the full unedited bodycam from each deputy, the arrest report or dispatch audio, send a request to the Marion County Sheriff's Office with the date and place: Nov. 11, 2026, North Williams Street, Dunnellon. Frequently asked questions

What happened at the Dunnellon traffic stop?

According to the Marion County Sheriff's Office, a deputy stopped a car on North Williams Street in Dunnellon, Florida, on Nov. 11, 2026, after clocking it at 52 mph in a 35 mph zone. MCSO says the driver, identified as Dawn Collier, 66, repeatedly refused to provide her license and registration. After a supervisor arrived, deputies broke the driver's side window to get her out. MCSO says she punched and kicked at deputies before she was stunned with a Taser and arrested. She is presumed innocent unless proven guilty in court.

What was the driver charged with?

The sheriff's office said she was charged with two counts of battery on a law enforcement officer, resisting an officer with violence, and resisting an officer without violence (obstruction). MCSO described that as three felony charges and one misdemeanor charge. These are allegations, not convictions.

Do you have to show your license when pulled over in Florida?

Yes. Florida Statute 322.15 says every licensed driver must have the license in immediate possession while driving and must present or submit it on demand of a law enforcement officer. A digital license is allowed, but if the officer can't immediately verify it, the officer can ask for the printed card. Section 320.0605 separately requires the registration to be in the vehicle and shown on demand. This is general information, not legal advice.

Is refusing to show your license a crime in Florida?

Failing to display a license under 322.15 is, by itself, a noncriminal traffic infraction, and the officer can require your fingerprints on the citation. But a refusal can lead to other issues. Refusing to sign and accept a citation for a criminal traffic violation or a violation that requires a mandatory hearing is a second-degree misdemeanor under 318.14(3), and resisting or obstructing an officer in the lawful execution of a legal duty is a first-degree misdemeanor under 843.02. Whether a refusal crosses that line depends on the facts.

What is resisting with violence in Florida?

Section 843.01 makes it a third-degree felony to knowingly and willfully resist, obstruct or oppose an officer by offering or doing violence to the officer. A third-degree felony carries up to five years in prison and a $5,000 fine. Resisting without violence under 843.02 is a first-degree misdemeanor, up to one year and $1,000.
