Lee County PIT Maneuver Bodycam: What Is a PIT Maneuver, and Is It Legal?

Oct 23, 20267 min readBy The Chest Mount

Lee County, FL deputies used a PIT maneuver to end a North Fort Myers chase, and the bodycam caught the driver asking for a cigarette. What a PIT maneuver is, when it's legal, and what Florida's fleeing (316.1935) and reckless driving (316.192) laws say. General information, not legal advice.

Share Share on X Facebook Reddit

Most police chases end one of a few ways: the driver stops, the pursuit is called off, or officers force the vehicle to stop. In North Fort Myers, Florida, this week's viral bodycam shows the third option, a PIT maneuver that tipped a pickup truck onto its side. Then came the line that sent the clip around the internet: "Can I get a cigarette?"

This explainer covers what the Lee County Sheriff's Office and news outlets have reported, what a PIT maneuver actually is, whether it's legal, and what Florida's fleeing and eluding and reckless driving laws say. It's general information, not legal advice. Every charge mentioned here is an allegation. The driver is presumed innocent unless proven guilty in court.

What happened in North Fort Myers, according to the sheriff's office

The facts below come from the Lee County Sheriff's Office (LCSO) as reported by Officer.com (Oct. 23, 2026), WINK News (Oct. 21), WBBH / Gulf Coast News (Oct. 21) and WFLA (Oct. 22).

  • The search. Around 3 a.m. on Sunday, Oct. 18, 2026, deputies on Orange Grove Boulevard were helping the Fort Myers Police Department look for a vehicle that had fled a traffic stop into North Fort Myers.
  • The driving. According to LCSO, as the truck approached the intersection of Orange Grove Boulevard and Pondella Road, the driver went around a parked vehicle and through a red light, nearly hitting another truck. When deputies tried to stop it, the driver accelerated, lost traction and nearly struck another vehicle, then drove over the median and into oncoming traffic.
  • The PIT maneuver. The sheriff's office says deputies performed a PIT maneuver "to prevent [the driver] from continuing to drive recklessly and potentially cause a deadly crash." The impact knocked the truck onto its side.
  • The bodycam. Officer.com reports the footage shows deputies approaching with guns drawn. The driver asks, "Can I get a cigarette?" A deputy tells him to get out of the truck, and he apologizes as he climbs out through the driver's side window.
  • The charges. LCSO says deputies found what it called "trafficking amounts" of cocaine and other drugs in the truck. According to WINK, WBBH and WFLA, the charges are possession of a controlled substance, possession of marijuana, trafficking in cocaine, possession of drug paraphernalia, fleeing and eluding, and reckless driving.

None of the reports we found mention injuries. LCSO released the bodycam on its official social media. We don't host or embed the footage. Watch it through the sheriff's office or the linked news reports.

What is a PIT maneuver?

PIT stands for precision immobilization technique. (Some agencies and court records call it the precision intervention technique.) The Florida Highway Patrol's pursuit policy defines it as "a specific, technical maneuver using a patrol vehicle to physically force a fleeing vehicle from a course of travel in order to stop it."

In plain terms, the patrol car makes controlled contact with the fleeing vehicle to turn it out of its path. The Supreme Court's opinion in Scott v. Harris (below) quotes a description of the PIT as a maneuver "which causes the fleeing vehicle to spin to a stop." How a vehicle reacts depends on speed, the road and the vehicle itself, which is why FHP's policy lists "type of fleeing vehicle" as a safety factor. In this case, the truck ended up on its side.

Is the PIT maneuver legal?

Yes, in general, but it's judged case by case. Three layers matter.

1. The Constitution. Courts treat stopping a car by force as a Fourth Amendment "seizure," so the question is whether the force was objectively reasonable. The leading Supreme Court case is Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372 (2007). A Georgia deputy ended a high-speed chase by pushing the fleeing car with his bumper (the opinion notes he decided against a PIT because of the speed), and the driver was badly injured. The Court held that "a police officer's attempt to terminate a dangerous high-speed car chase that threatens the lives of innocent bystanders does not violate the Fourth Amendment, even when it places the fleeing motorist at risk of serious injury or death." Two concurring justices stressed that this is not a mechanical rule. The facts still matter, including whether there was a safer way to stop the vehicle.

2. Agency policy. Each agency decides who can perform a PIT and when. The Florida Highway Patrol's policy 17.05 (revised March 2024) is one public Florida example. It:

  • Calls the PIT "the primary technique employed by the Division to terminate a pursuit"
  • Says it must be used according to training
  • Lists factors to weigh first: road condition, visibility, traffic volume, pedestrians, the type of fleeing vehicle and its occupants
  • Calls it "a use of control" that must be "objectively reasonable," and warns that "the greater the speed, the greater the likelihood of injury or death"
  • Bars it on motorcycles and vehicles with fewer than four tires unless deadly force is authorized
  • Requires a supervisory use-of-control report every time a PIT is used

That's FHP's policy, not the Lee County Sheriff's Office's. We couldn't find LCSO's pursuit policy published online. It can be requested as a public record.

3. Review afterward. Policies like FHP's require paperwork after every use, and Scott v. Harris itself began as a civil-rights lawsuit by the injured driver. Whether a particular maneuver was reasonable is decided on its own facts.

Fleeing and eluding in Florida: section 316.1935

Florida's law is section 316.1935, Florida Statutes. In short:

  • Subsection (1): A driver who knows they've been ordered to stop by an officer and "willfully" fails to stop, or stops and then flees, commits a third-degree felony.
  • Subsection (2): Willfully fleeing a marked patrol vehicle with siren and lights on is also a third-degree felony.
  • Subsection (3): It becomes a second-degree felony when the fleeing involves high speed or "wanton disregard for the safety of persons or property," and a first-degree felony with a 3-year mandatory minimum if it causes serious bodily injury or death.
  • Penalties beyond prison: a driver license revocation of 1 to 5 years, no withholding of adjudication, and the vehicle can be seized as contraband.

As our Winter Springs explainer covers, the words knowledge and willfully carry real weight. Whether a driver knew they were being ordered to stop is a question of fact for a court.

Reckless driving in Florida: section 316.192

Section 316.192 defines reckless driving as driving "in willful or wanton disregard for the safety of persons or property." It also says "fleeing a law enforcement officer in a motor vehicle is reckless driving per se."

  • A first conviction is punishable by up to 90 days in jail and/or a fine of $25 to $500.
  • A second or later conviction: up to 6 months and/or $50 to $1,000.
  • If it damages someone else's property or person, it's a first-degree misdemeanor. Serious bodily injury makes it a third-degree felony.

So, is reckless driving a felony in Florida? Under this statute, only when it causes serious bodily injury to someone else. Otherwise the penalties are the jail and fine limits above, or a first-degree misdemeanor when it damages another person or their property. This is general information. Anyone facing a charge should talk to a Florida attorney.

Why this bodycam is worth watching closely

The cigarette line made the clip go viral, but what matters more is what it does and doesn't show. A bodycam starts recording at the officer's chest. It usually won't show the full driving that led up to the PIT, which is exactly what decides whether the maneuver was reasonable. Dashcam video, dispatch audio and the agency's use-of-control report fill that gap. Our guide on how to watch police bodycam footage critically goes through what to look for.

How to request the records

Florida's public records law covers sheriff's offices. You can send a written request to the agency's records custodian for bodycam and dashcam video, the pursuit or use-of-control report, or the agency's pursuit policy itself. Active criminal investigative information can be withheld while a case is open. Our FOIA letter generator drafts the request for you, and our guide on how police bodycam footage becomes public explains exemptions, fees and appeals.
