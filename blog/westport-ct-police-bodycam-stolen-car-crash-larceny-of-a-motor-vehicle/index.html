Westport, CT Police Bodycam: Stolen Car Crash, K-9 Search and What Larceny of a Motor Vehicle Means

Nov 9, 20267 min readBy The Chest Mount

Westport, CT police released bodycam of a search and four arrests after a license plate reader flagged a stolen Genesis G70 that later crashed on Greens Farms Road. How CT's larceny of a motor vehicle (53a-119c) and interfering with an officer (53a-167a) laws work, plus plate readers and K-9 tracks. General information, not legal advice.

Share Share on X Facebook Reddit

A stolen-car alert from a roadside camera, a crash into a tree, muddy marshland and a police dog: that's how Westport police describe the morning of Nov. 5, 2026. Body camera footage of the search and arrests is now circulating after News 12 Connecticut published it on Nov. 9.

The case also raises questions people search for every day: what does "larceny of a motor vehicle" mean in Connecticut, and is "interfering with an officer" a felony? This explainer covers what police have said and how those laws work. It's general information, not legal advice. Everyone mentioned is presumed innocent. The charges below are allegations, not convictions.

What happened, according to police

The facts below come from the Westport Police Department's account as reported by the Westport Journal (Nov. 6), FOX61 (Nov. 7) and News 12 Connecticut (Nov. 9). The department posts releases on its press releases page.

  • The alert. Overnight into Thursday, Nov. 5, a stationary license plate reader flagged a 2026 Genesis G70 entering town on Wilton Road near the Merritt Parkway. Police say the car had been reported stolen out of Enfield. Officers searched the area but couldn't find it at first.
  • The sighting. Later that morning, an officer spotted the car on Compo Road South near Compo Beach Road. Police say the driver saw the marked cruiser and sped away, out of the officer's sight.
  • The crash. Thinking the car might head for I-95, the officer drove east on Greens Farms Road and found it crashed into a tree near Prospect Road, with disabling damage.
  • The search. One person was seen running and others were heard nearby. The area is open space and wetland marsh, and police say several shoes got stuck in the mud, which gave clues about how many people had been in the car. Westport officers, Fairfield police and Connecticut State Police set up a perimeter, and a K-9 unit from Bridgeport police helped with the search.
  • The arrests. Police say a K-9 track led to one man hiding near the crash site. A second was caught on the I-95 northbound ramp at Exit 18. A third was found at a diner in Fairfield after 911 callers reported a man walking without shoes on Oxford Road. About 45 minutes after police sent a press advisory, a Greens Farms Road resident reported an injured man on his front porch, and police allege he was the car's fourth occupant.

All four men, ages 18 to 22, from Hartford and Middletown, were charged with larceny of a motor vehicle (first offense) and interfering with an officer. Police say one also faces reckless driving and narcotics charges, another a possession charge, and a third a first-degree failure to appear charge tied to an earlier warrant. Bonds ranged from $75,000 to $900,000. None were posted, and all four were taken to Stamford Superior Court for arraignment on Nov. 6.

We don't host or embed the footage. Watch it through News 12 Connecticut's report. News 12 says it shows the arrests of several suspects after the crash. We're also not repeating the men's names: the case is at the arraignment stage, and this post is about the law, not the people.

How a license plate reader flags a stolen car

An automated license plate reader (ALPR) is a fixed or mobile camera that turns images of plates into computer-readable data. When a plate matches a list of vehicles of interest, like cars reported stolen, the system alerts officers. That's what police say started this case: a fixed camera on Wilton Road, hours before anyone saw the car.

Connecticut changed the rules this fall. Public Act 26-14, effective Oct. 1, 2026, limits when police and other public agencies can use ALPR systems and data. According to the Office of Legislative Research's summary, the law:

  • lets officers enter plate numbers to help recover a stolen vehicle, find a missing or endangered person, catch someone with an outstanding warrant, or support a specific active criminal investigation based on reasonable suspicion,
  • generally limits how long agencies can keep ALPR data to 21 days, and
  • allows longer retention for warrants, court orders and evidence in an active criminal case, with a supervisor's approval and a record of the case number.

An ALPR hit is a lead, not proof of who was driving or what anyone knew. Those questions are for court.

Larceny of a motor vehicle: Conn. Gen. Stat. 53a-119c

Connecticut created a separate car-theft offense in 2022. Under section 53a-119c, a person is guilty of larceny of a motor vehicle when they commit larceny, as defined in section 53a-119, and the property is a motor vehicle. In short, larceny means wrongfully taking, obtaining or withholding property from its owner with the intent to deprive the owner of it or to appropriate it to themselves or someone else.

The statute also lists evidence that counts as "prima facie" proof that a person in control or possession of the car knew or should have known it was stolen and had larcenous intent: (1) forcible entry, (2) a forcibly removed ignition, or (3) an altered or removed vehicle identification number. Prima facie evidence can still be challenged in court.

| Offense under 53a-119c(c) | Classification | Maximum prison term | |---|---|---| | First offense | Class E felony | Up to 3 years | | Second offense | Class D felony | Up to 5 years | | Subsequent offense | Class B felony | 1 to 20 years |

Prison ranges come from section 53a-35a. The maximum fine for a class E felony is $3,500 under section 53a-41. Connecticut also has a separate, lesser offense, using a motor vehicle without the owner's permission (section 53a-119b), a class A misdemeanor for a first offense.

Police didn't say which of the four was driving, and charging everyone in a car with larceny doesn't mean each one took it. The state has to prove each person's own knowledge and intent.

Interfering with an officer: Conn. Gen. Stat. 53a-167a

Section 53a-167a says a person is guilty of interfering with an officer when they "obstructs, resists, hinders or endangers any peace officer or firefighter in the performance of such peace officer's or firefighter's duties."

  • Penalty. It's a class A misdemeanor, punishable by up to one year in jail (section 53a-36) and a fine of up to $2,000 (section 53a-42).
  • When it's a felony. It becomes a class D felony only if the violation causes the death or serious physical injury of another person.

The court decisions noted under the statute fill in the details:

  • The officers must have been acting in the performance of their duties.
  • The Connecticut Supreme Court has said the statute covers conduct that hinders police "irrespective of whether offending conduct is active or passive," including, in some cases, refusing to give identification after a lawful stop.
  • The Appellate Court upheld a conviction where a person gave a false name at a scene and again at booking.
  • Courts have also said that not identifying yourself instantly, or making statements like "this isn't Russia," isn't automatically interference.

Running from a crash scene after police arrive is a common basis for this charge. Notably, none of the reported charges here is Connecticut's separate engaging-in-pursuit offense, section 14-223(b), which covers speeding up to escape an officer using lights or a siren. One man faces reckless driving under section 14-222, which covers driving at a speed that endangers others or at more than 85 mph. Florida and Oregon handle fleeing differently; see our Winter Springs, FL and Beaverton, OR explainers.

Why K-9 tracks follow bailouts

When people run from a crashed car into woods or marsh, officers often set a perimeter first, blocking roads and exits, and then bring in a tracking dog. A K-9 follows the scent trail from the point where someone left the car, which is why tracks often start right at the crash. Departments often borrow dogs from neighboring agencies, as Westport did with Bridgeport. Police say the shoes left in the mud also helped officers work out how many people they were looking for, and calls from the public led to two of the arrests.

If you're ever in a car that's involved in a police stop or crash, staying put and keeping your hands visible is the safest choice. Running adds risk and can add charges. How to request the bodycam in Connecticut

Connecticut's Freedom of Information Act (Conn. Gen. Stat. 1-200 and following) gives the public a right to inspect public records, including police records, with exemptions and special rules for body camera recordings. To request footage, send a written request to the department's records division with the date, location and any case number. Frequently asked questions

What happened in the Westport, CT stolen car crash?

According to the Westport Police Department, as reported by News 12 Connecticut, the Westport Journal and FOX61, a stationary license plate reader flagged a 2026 Genesis G70 reported stolen out of Enfield entering Westport overnight on Nov. 5, 2026. Later that morning an officer saw it on Compo Road South; police say it sped away and was found crashed on Greens Farms Road near Prospect Road. Several people ran, and four men, ages 18 to 22, were arrested after a multi-agency search that included a Bridgeport police K-9. These are allegations, and all four are presumed innocent.

What is larceny of a motor vehicle in Connecticut?

Under Conn. Gen. Stat. 53a-119c, a person is guilty of larceny of a motor vehicle when they commit larceny, as defined in section 53a-119, and the property is a motor vehicle. It is a class E felony for a first offense, a class D felony for a second offense and a class B felony after that. This is general information, not legal advice.

Is interfering with an officer a felony in Connecticut?

Usually not. Conn. Gen. Stat. 53a-167a makes interfering with an officer a class A misdemeanor, punishable by up to one year in jail and a fine of up to $2,000. It becomes a class D felony only if the violation causes the death or serious physical injury of another person.

Can giving police a false name count as interfering with an officer in CT?

Connecticut courts have said it can. The case annotations to section 53a-167a on the General Assembly's website note an Appellate Court decision finding enough evidence for a conviction where a person gave police a false name at a scene and again at booking. Courts have also said that simply not identifying yourself instantly, or making declaratory statements, isn't automatically interference.

How do license plate readers flag stolen cars in Connecticut?

A license plate reader camera converts images of plates into data and checks them against lists of vehicles of interest, such as cars reported stolen. A Connecticut law that took effect Oct. 1, 2026 (Public Act 26-14) limits how police can use these systems and generally caps how long agencies can keep the data at 21 days, with exceptions such as evidence in an active criminal investigation.
