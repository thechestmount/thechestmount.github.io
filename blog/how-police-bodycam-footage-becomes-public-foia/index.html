How Police Bodycam Footage Becomes Public: FOIA and Public-Records Requests Explained

Oct 20, 20263 min readBy The Chest Mount

How real police bodycam footage gets released: federal FOIA vs. state public-records laws, how to request footage, common exemptions, redactions, fees and appeals. General information, not legal advice.

Share Share on X Facebook Reddit

Every full video on The Chest Mount starts the same way: footage that a police department recorded and later released. Viewers often ask how that works. Do agencies post it? Do you have to sue? Is it legal to share? This guide explains, in general terms, how police bodycam footage becomes public through public-records requests, often called FOIA requests.

This is general information, not legal advice. Public-records laws vary a lot from state to state.

Federal FOIA vs. state public-records laws

People say "FOIA request" for any records request, but there are really two layers:

  • The federal Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) gives the public a right to request records from federal agencies. The government's official portal, FOIA.gov, explains how it works, and the U.S. Department of Justice's Office of Information Policy oversees compliance.
  • State public-records laws cover state and local agencies, including city police departments and county sheriff's offices. Most bodycam footage you see online, including ours, comes through these state laws, not the federal FOIA.

Many states model their laws on the federal FOIA, and some even use the name (Illinois's law, for example, is the Illinois Freedom of Information Act). Others call them "open records" or "public records" acts. The Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press publishes a state-by-state Open Government Guide that compares how each state's law works.

Bodycam footage often has its own rules

Body-worn cameras are newer than most public-records laws, so many states have passed specific rules about bodycam recordings. These cover when footage can be released, how long it's kept, and what must be redacted. In Illinois, for example, the Law Enforcement Officer-Worn Body Camera Act sets rules for how agencies use and retain recordings, alongside the state's FOIA.

The practical result is that the same request can get very different answers in different states, or even from different agencies in the same state.

How a request typically works

The exact process varies, but most requests follow the same basic steps:

  1. Identify the agency. Footage belongs to the department whose officers recorded it, so a traffic stop by a state trooper and one by a city officer go to different agencies.
  2. Be specific. The more details you include, the easier it is to find the right video: date, approximate time, location, incident or report number, and the officers or units involved if you know them.
  3. Submit the request in writing. Many agencies have an online portal or a designated FOIA officer. Keep a copy and note the date.
  4. Wait for the response. Deadlines are set by state law. Illinois's FOIA, for example, generally gives public bodies five business days to respond, with a possible extension. Other states differ.
  5. Pay any allowed fees. Some agencies charge for copying, media or, in some states, time spent reviewing and redacting video.
  6. Receive the footage, or a denial explaining why. A denial should cite the specific exemption the agency is relying on.

Common reasons footage is withheld or redacted

Public-records laws balance transparency against other interests. Common exemptions include:

  • Ongoing investigations or prosecutions. Agencies may delay release while a case is active.
  • Personal privacy. Bystanders, victims, witnesses and the inside of private homes are often protected.
  • Minors. Juvenile information is commonly withheld or blurred.
  • Medical or sensitive personal information.
  • Officer or public safety concerns in specific situations.

That's why many released videos have blurred faces, muted audio or black boxes over screens and paperwork. Those redactions are part of the legal release process, not edits made to hide something.

If a request is denied

Most laws give requesters a way to appeal. In Illinois, the Attorney General's office includes a Public Access Counselor that reviews FOIA disputes. Other states use an ombudsman, a records commission or the courts. Groups like the National Freedom of Information Coalition support open-government work and point to state-level resources.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use the federal FOIA to get local police bodycam footage?

Usually not. The federal Freedom of Information Act covers federal agencies. City police and county sheriffs are covered by state public-records laws, which work similarly but have their own rules.

Does a police department have to release bodycam footage?

It depends on state law. Many states treat bodycam video as a public record but allow agencies to withhold or redact it in some situations, such as ongoing investigations or privacy concerns.

Why are faces blurred in some bodycam videos?

Agencies often redact footage before release to protect privacy, for example bystanders, minors, victims or the inside of private homes. Some creators also blur faces or personal details before publishing.

What can I do if my request is denied?

Most public-records laws have an appeal process. In Illinois, for example, the Attorney General's Public Access Counselor reviews FOIA disputes. Other states have their own appeal routes, including court.
