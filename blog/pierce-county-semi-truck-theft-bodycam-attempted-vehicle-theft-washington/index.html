Pierce County Bodycam: Man Starts a Semi but Can't Release the Brakes, and What Attempted Vehicle Theft Means in Washington

Nov 13, 20267 min readBy The Chest Mount

Pierce County, WA deputies released bodycam and dashcam of a man they say started a farm's semi-truck in Puyallup but couldn't get it moving. What the sheriff's office and news reports say, how Washington's attempt, motor vehicle theft and taking-without-permission laws work, and why big rigs don't roll until the air brakes release. General information, not legal advice.

Share Share on X Facebook Reddit

Most stolen-vehicle videos end with a chase. This one ended in a farm lot, with the engine running and the truck going nowhere. Body camera and dash camera video released by the Pierce County Sheriff's Office in Washington shows deputies ordering a man out of a semi-truck's cab in the Puyallup area, then removing him and taking him into custody. The sheriff's office says he had started the rig but couldn't figure out how to get it moving.

This explainer covers what the sheriff's office and news outlets have reported, and then answers the questions people are searching for: is attempted vehicle theft a felony in Washington, what "taking a motor vehicle without permission" means, and why a semi doesn't roll just because the engine is on. It's general information, not legal advice. The man was arrested on allegations only, the sheriff's office hasn't named him, and he is presumed innocent unless proven guilty in court.

What happened in Puyallup, according to the sheriff's office

The facts below come from the Pierce County Sheriff's Office's statements and video, as reported by KIRO 7, MyNorthwest and Police1.

  • The call. At about 2:15 a.m. on Wednesday, Nov. 4, 2026, a security guard at a farm in the Puyallup area called 911 to report a theft in progress. The guard said a man had gotten into a semi-truck and started it after smoking something inside the cab.
  • What deputies found. When deputies arrived, the sheriff's office says the 24-year-old man was in the cab with the engine running, turning the steering wheel and "apparently trying to figure out how to get the massive rig moving."
  • The removal. The video captures deputies ordering him out ("Out. Step out. Get out."). The sheriff's office says he refused repeated commands, so deputies removed him from the cab, and a Taser was deployed after he continued resisting.
  • The arrest. He was arrested for attempted motor vehicle theft and resisting arrest. Deputies say they found drug paraphernalia among his belongings and believe he was under the influence of narcotics. Those are the deputies' allegations and observations, not test results or court findings.
  • The truck. It never left the parking lot. "We're especially grateful he didn't figure out how to operate the brakes," the sheriff's office wrote, adding that a stolen semi "could have caused some serious damage and put lives at risk."

The sheriff's office posted the footage around Nov. 6, and it spread after KIRO 7 and MyNorthwest covered it on Nov. 9 and Police1 on Nov. 11. Watch it through the linked reports. We don't host or embed it.

What the bodycam shows, and what it doesn't

The video shows the end of the encounter: commands, the removal from the cab, and the arrest. It doesn't show what happened before deputies arrived, what the man intended, or whether he was impaired. Those are exactly the questions a court would decide if the case is charged. An officer's remark at the scene, like the deputy noting afterward that the truck was "an automatic, too," is an observation, not evidence of anyone's plan. Is attempted vehicle theft a felony in Washington?

Washington handles this in two steps: the crime that was attempted, and the attempt statute.

The underlying crime. RCW 9A.56.065 makes theft of a motor vehicle a class B felony. Theft itself is defined in RCW 9A.56.020 and generally means wrongfully obtaining or exerting unauthorized control over someone else's property with intent to deprive them of it.

The attempt. Under RCW 9A.28.020, a person is guilty of attempt if, with intent to commit a specific crime, he or she does "any act which is a substantial step toward the commission of that crime." The same statute steps the grade down by one level: an attempt to commit a class B felony is a class C felony. It also says it is no defense that the crime turned out to be impossible to complete under the circumstances.

That's why "attempted" matters in a case like this. Because the sheriff's office says the truck never left the lot, the allegation is that the theft wasn't completed. Whether getting into the cab and starting the engine was a "substantial step," and whether the person intended to steal the truck, are questions for a prosecutor to prove and a court to decide.

Maximum penalties. RCW 9A.20.021 caps a class B felony at 10 years and a $20,000 fine, and a class C felony at five years and a $10,000 fine. Those are ceilings, not typical outcomes. Actual sentences in Washington run through the state's sentencing guidelines and depend heavily on criminal history.

Taking a motor vehicle without permission (TMVWOP)

Washington also has a separate pair of offenses that people often confuse with vehicle theft:

  • Second degree, RCW 9A.56.075. Without the owner's permission, intentionally taking or driving away a vehicle that belongs to someone else, or voluntarily riding in one knowing it was unlawfully taken. It's a class C felony.
  • First degree, RCW 9A.56.070. Taking a vehicle without permission plus an aggravating fact, such as changing its identification numbers, removing parts to sell, exporting it for profit, intending to sell it, or being part of a theft-for-profit conspiracy. It's a class B felony.

The practical difference from theft is mostly about intent. Theft requires intent to deprive the owner of the property. Taking without permission focuses on the unauthorized taking or driving itself. Which charge fits a given case, if any, is up to the prosecutor based on the evidence.

Resisting arrest in Washington

RCW 9A.76.040 says a person is guilty of resisting arrest if he or she "intentionally prevents or attempts to prevent a peace officer from lawfully arresting him or her." It's a misdemeanor, which under RCW 9A.20.021 carries up to 90 days in jail and a $1,000 fine. The statute includes the word "lawfully," so the legality of the arrest can matter. If you're ever in a situation like this, the safest course is to comply and challenge the stop later in court. Our Know Your Rights page and the detained vs. arrested explainer cover the basics.

Why a running semi doesn't just drive off

The sheriff's office's comment about the brakes points to a real feature of heavy trucks. Many semis use air brakes. On those systems, the parking brakes are spring brakes: strong springs hold the brakes on, and air pressure is what releases them. When the truck has been sitting, the springs keep it locked in place.

Starting the engine runs the air compressor, but the truck still won't roll freely until there's enough pressure in the system and the driver releases the parking brake control, which on many trucks is a push-pull knob on the dashboard. Someone who has never driven a truck with air brakes may not know any of that. That's a large part of why searches like "semi truck air brakes not releasing" and "semi truck air brake knobs" are so common, and it's one reason CDL testing includes a separate air brake knowledge test.

We're describing how these systems generally work, not the specific truck in this video, which hasn't been publicly identified.

How to request the records in Washington

The sheriff's office chose to release this video. Other records, such as the full bodycam from each deputy, the incident report and the 911 call, can be requested under Washington's Public Records Act (chapter 42.56 RCW), which has specific rules for body-worn camera recordings. Records tied to an open case can be withheld or redacted. Send the request to the Pierce County Sheriff's Office with the date, time and location: Nov. 4, 2026, about 2:15 a.m., a farm in the Puyallup area. Frequently asked questions

What happened in the Puyallup semi-truck bodycam?

According to the Pierce County Sheriff's Office, as reported by KIRO 7, MyNorthwest and Police1, deputies were called to a farm in the Puyallup area at about 2:15 a.m. on Nov. 4, 2026, after a security guard reported a man had gotten into a semi-truck and started it. Deputies say they found a 24-year-old man in the cab with the engine running, turning the steering wheel. He didn't get out when ordered, deputies removed him from the cab and used a Taser when he kept resisting. The truck never left the lot.

What was the man arrested for?

The sheriff's office says he was arrested for attempted motor vehicle theft and resisting arrest. Those are arrest allegations, not convictions. The sheriff's office hasn't released his name, and as of Nov. 13, 2026 we haven't found a public report of formal charges. He is presumed innocent unless proven guilty in court.

Is attempted vehicle theft a felony in Washington?

Usually, yes. Theft of a motor vehicle is a class B felony under RCW 9A.56.065. Under Washington's attempt statute, RCW 9A.28.020, an attempt to commit a class B felony is a class C felony, which carries a maximum of five years and a $10,000 fine under RCW 9A.20.021. Actual sentences depend on the sentencing guidelines and criminal history. This is general information, not legal advice.

What is taking a motor vehicle without permission in the second degree?

Under RCW 9A.56.075, a person commits taking a motor vehicle without permission in the second degree if, without the owner's permission, he or she intentionally takes or drives away a vehicle that belongs to someone else, or knowingly rides in one that was unlawfully taken. It is a class C felony. The first-degree version, RCW 9A.56.070, adds aggravating facts such as intending to sell the vehicle or strip it for parts, and is a class B felony.

Why couldn't the truck move even with the engine running?

Many heavy trucks use air brakes with spring-applied parking brakes. The springs hold the brakes on until the engine's compressor builds enough air pressure and the driver releases the parking brake control, which on many trucks is a push-pull knob on the dash. Starting the engine alone doesn't release them. The sheriff's office said it was grateful the man didn't figure out the brakes.
