How to Watch Police Bodycam Footage Critically: 9 Things to Keep in Mind

Oct 20, 20264 min readBy The Chest Mount

Bodycam video feels like the full truth, but it isn't. Nine practical ways to watch police bodycam footage critically: camera angle, missing context, audio, editing, charges vs. convictions and more.

Share Share on X Facebook Reddit

Police bodycam footage feels like the final word. You're seeing what the officer saw, from the officer's chest, with no narrator. But anyone who's read the comments under a viral video knows two people can watch the same clip and come away completely sure of opposite things.

Here are nine practical habits for watching police bodycam footage more critically, whether you're watching our videos or anyone else's.

1. Remember where the camera is

A bodycam is usually mounted on the officer's chest (hence our name). That position has quirks:

  • It doesn't follow the officer's eyes. When an officer turns their head, the camera doesn't.
  • It's low and close, which can make movements look bigger, faster or more chaotic.
  • During a struggle, it's often blocked by an arm, the ground or another person.

That's why footage of physical encounters, like the floor struggle in our Marion, Illinois video, can be hard to read even after several viewings.

2. Ask what happened before the recording

Most videos start once officers arrive. You usually don't see the 911 call, the dispatcher's information, earlier visits or the history between the people involved. In the Marion footage, the officer mentions afterward that he'd told the man to leave "twice in the last few weeks." That context matters, and it isn't on camera.

Some cameras save a short buffer from before the officer hits record, often without audio. If the first seconds are silent, that's usually why.

3. Treat on-screen captions as summaries, not facts

Many channels, including ours, add an opening caption like "On [date], officers in [city] respond to..." Those captions summarize the call as it was reported. They're helpful context, but they aren't evidence that anything in them is true.

4. Listen carefully, and know that captions get words wrong

Audio in bodycam footage is often overlapping, muffled or distorted by wind and movement. Automatic captions frequently mishear words, including names and places. If a quote matters to your opinion, rewind and listen yourself.

5. Notice the edits

Ask yourself:

  • Are there jump cuts or skipped time?
  • Has music been added?
  • Are faces or screens blurred? That's often a legal redaction by the agency.
  • Is it a highlight clip or the full encounter?

Short clips are great for getting attention, but they compress context. That's why our Shorts always point back to the full video. For example, the Refused to Leave series links to the complete Marion footage.

6. Separate allegations from what you can see

People say a lot during tense encounters, and officers do too. Treat each statement as a claim by that person:

  • An officer saying "he grabbed my taser" is the officer's account.
  • A driver yelling "he kicked me in my face," as in our Peoria traffic stop, is the driver's account.

Sometimes the video confirms a claim, sometimes it contradicts it, and often it simply can't show it. Be honest about which of those you're looking at.

7. Remember that charges aren't convictions

You'll often hear officers list possible charges at the end of a video. Those are accusations. Guilt is decided in court, where evidence like other cameras, witness statements and reports is tested. Everyone shown in our videos is presumed innocent until proven guilty in a court of law.

8. Know the general legal standard for use of force

When people argue about whether force was justified, they're often using different yardsticks. U.S. courts generally judge police use of force by "objective reasonableness," from Graham v. Connor (1989): would a reasonable officer on the scene have acted that way, given the threat, the suspected offense and any resistance? Department policy can be stricter, and your own sense of what should happen is a separate, valid question. Keeping those three apart makes discussions much clearer.

If you're curious about the terms officers use, see what drive-stun means on a taser and detained vs. arrested.

9. Watch the aftermath

Some of the most revealing moments come after the action, when officers explain what happened to each other, check for injuries, or talk to witnesses. In the Marion video, the officer says, "I did not want it to go down like that." In the Buffalo Grove footage, an officer agrees to adjust handcuffs once the man settles down. Those moments add context that the dramatic middle doesn't.

Respect the real people involved

The people in these videos are real, and many have never been convicted of anything. Please don't search for, share or post personal information about anyone shown, and keep comments about the conduct, not the person's identity.

Put it into practice

Pick a full-length video from our library, watch it once for the story, then again with this checklist. You'll likely notice things you missed. Then tell us respectfully in the comments: did the second viewing change your opinion?

Frequently asked questions

Is bodycam footage always accurate?

It's an accurate recording of what the camera captured, but it's not the whole story. The angle, field of view, audio quality and what happened before recording started all limit what it can show.

Why do bodycam videos start in the middle of the action?

Many cameras keep a short buffer from before an officer presses record, often without audio. Agencies also release specific clips, and creators trim footage for length.

Does an arrest on camera mean someone is guilty?

No. An arrest is based on probable cause, and guilt is decided by a court. Everyone shown is presumed innocent unless proven guilty.

How can I tell if a bodycam video was edited?

Look for jump cuts, gaps in timestamps, added captions, blur and music. Reputable channels say when footage is edited. Our videos may be trimmed for length but are not altered to change context.
