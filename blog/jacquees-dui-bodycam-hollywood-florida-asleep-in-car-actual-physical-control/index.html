Jacquees DUI Bodycam: Can You Get a DUI Asleep in a Parked Car in Florida?

Nov 18, 20266 min readBy The Chest Mount

Bodycam from R&B singer Jacquees' Sept. 27 arrest in Hollywood, FL went viral after TMZ published it Nov. 17. What police and news reports say, what 'actual physical control' means under Florida DUI law, and how battery on a first responder is charged. General information, not legal advice.

Share Share on X Facebook Reddit

R&B singer Jacquees dared police to release the bodycam from his September arrest in Florida. On Nov. 17, 2026, TMZ published that footage, credited to the Hollywood Police Department, and it spread fast on X. The clip shows officers and firefighters around his Jeep, a tense stretch inside an ambulance, and the singer later appearing to doze off in the back of a patrol car.

The case also raises one of the most searched DUI questions: can you get a DUI if you're asleep in a parked car? In Florida the answer turns on two words in the statute, "actual physical control." This explainer covers what police and news outlets have reported, then walks through Florida's DUI, battery-on-a-first-responder and resisting laws. It's general information, not legal advice. Jacquees has denied the allegations and is presumed innocent unless proven guilty in court.

What happened in Hollywood, according to police

The account below comes from the arrest report and footage as reported by TMZ, AtlantaFi and AllHipHop.

  • The call. Just before 8 a.m. on Sept. 27, 2026, Hollywood police responded to a report of a man passed out inside a vehicle near Hollywood Boulevard and North 62nd Avenue.
  • The Jeep. Officers say they found the singer, whose legal name is Rodriquez Jacquees Broadnax, 32, asleep in his white Jeep Gladiator. Police say they tapped on the windows and Hollywood Fire Rescue crews shook the vehicle, but he didn't respond, so firefighters used a window punch to get inside. An officer noted an odor of alcohol.
  • The ambulance. He was placed on a stretcher and moved into a rescue truck. According to the report as described by AtlantaFi, he said he needed to urinate and told an officer to move, then punched the officer's arm when the officer put a hand on him to keep him from leaving the back of the truck. Reports say an officer then struck him on the side of the face with a forearm, rolled him to the floor of the ambulance and arrested him.
  • The charges. He was arrested on DUI, battery on an officer, firefighter or EMT, and resisting an officer without violence. Bond was set at $3,500, and he was released from the Broward County jail the next day.
  • His response. Leaving jail, he told reporters the accusations were false and that police would have to show the bodycam. According to AllHipHop, citing WPLG Local 10, he said his home was across the street and denied physically resisting. AtlantaFi reports he has pleaded not guilty. As of Nov. 18, 2026, we haven't found a public report of how the case has been resolved.

What the footage shows, and what it doesn't

The version TMZ published runs six minutes and 34 seconds. As AllHipHop points out, its on-screen clock jumps between sections, so it's an edited compilation, not an unbroken record. Parts of the ambulance encounter are blocked from the camera's view. The video also includes an insult and offensive language directed at an officer, and the forearm strike is brief.

That matters for how you read it. A bodycam clip can show what one camera saw. It can't show the moments that were cut, what anyone's blood or breath alcohol level was, or whether the singer was in a condition a court would call impaired. Those are questions for the evidence and the court. Can you get a DUI asleep in a parked car in Florida?

Florida's DUI law, section 316.193, applies when a person is "driving or in actual physical control of a vehicle" and either:

  • is under the influence of alcohol or certain drugs "to the extent that the person's normal faculties are impaired," or
  • has a blood or breath alcohol level of 0.08 or more.

The key part is "actual physical control." Florida's standard criminal jury instruction for DUI says it means the person "must be physically in or on the vehicle and have the capability to operate the vehicle, regardless of whether [he] [she] is actually operating the vehicle at the time." So in Florida, a DUI charge doesn't require that anyone saw the car moving.

Courts apply that definition to the specific facts. In State v. Fitzgerald (Fla. 2d DCA 2011), an officer found a woman in the driver's seat of a stopped car with the keys in her hand, and the appeals court reinstated a DUI charge a trial judge had thrown out, because the state could try to prove she had the capability to operate the car. The factors that usually come up include:

  • Where the person was sitting. Driver's seat versus back seat or passenger seat.
  • The keys. In the ignition, in a hand or pocket, or out of reach.
  • Whether the vehicle could run. Engine on or off, lights on, and whether the car was operable.
  • Where the vehicle was. In a travel lane or intersection, on the shoulder, or in a parking space.

None of those factors decides a case alone, and different states use different tests. Searches like "can you get a DUI for sleeping in your car in California" show why: the answer isn't the same everywhere. What reports say about the Jeep here (the singer asleep inside, near a busy road) are allegations the state would have to prove, along with impairment, beyond a reasonable doubt.

Penalties. For a first DUI conviction, section 316.193(2)(a) sets a fine of $500 to $1,000 and up to six months in jail. Higher blood alcohol levels, crashes, injuries and prior convictions raise those numbers, and a conviction also brings license consequences.

Battery on a firefighter, EMT or officer

Florida doesn't have a separate "hit a paramedic" crime. Instead, section 784.07 reclassifies an ordinary battery when it's knowingly committed on a law enforcement officer, a firefighter, an emergency medical care provider and several other listed workers while they're performing official duties. A simple battery, normally a first-degree misdemeanor, becomes a third-degree felony. Under sections 775.082 and 775.083, that carries a maximum of five years in prison and a $5,000 fine.

Two details often matter in these cases. The person has to knowingly commit the battery on someone in one of those roles, and the worker has to be engaged in official duties. Florida's battery statute also covers any intentional touching against someone's will, not just a hard strike. How the law applies to the ambulance moment in this video is for a court to decide.

Resisting an officer without violence

Section 843.02 makes it a first-degree misdemeanor to resist, obstruct or oppose an officer "in the lawful execution of any legal duty" without offering or doing violence. The maximum is one year in jail and a $1,000 fine. Because the duty has to be lawful, the legality of what officers were doing can become part of the defense. If you're ever in a situation like this, the safest course is to stay calm, comply, and challenge the stop later in court. How to request the records in Florida

Florida's public records law, chapter 119, is one of the broadest in the country, but bodycam footage and records tied to an active criminal case can be withheld or redacted while the case is open. To ask for the full bodycam from each officer, the incident report or the 911 call, send a request to the Hollywood Police Department with the date, time and place: Sept. 27, 2026, shortly before 8 a.m., Hollywood Boulevard and North 62nd Avenue. Fire rescue records may need a separate request to the city. Can you get a DUI for sleeping in your car in Florida?

It's possible. Florida's DUI statute, section 316.193, covers anyone who is 'driving or in actual physical control of a vehicle' while impaired or at 0.08 or above. Florida's standard jury instruction defines actual physical control as being physically in or on the vehicle with the capability to operate it, whether or not the person is actually operating it at the time. Courts look at the whole situation, such as where the keys were, which seat the person was in and whether the vehicle could run. This is general information, not legal advice.

Is battery on a firefighter or EMT a felony in Florida?

Under section 784.07, a battery knowingly committed on a law enforcement officer, firefighter or emergency medical care provider who is performing official duties is reclassified from a first-degree misdemeanor to a third-degree felony, which carries a maximum of five years and a $5,000 fine. Prosecutors must prove each element, including that the person knew the victim's role.

The maximum is one year in jail and a $1,000 fine. The duty has to be lawful, so the legality of the officer's actions can matter in court.
