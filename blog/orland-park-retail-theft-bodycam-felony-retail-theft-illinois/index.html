Orland Park Retail Theft Bodycam: When Is Shoplifting a Felony in Illinois?

Nov 25, 20267 min readBy The Chest Mount

Orland Park, IL bodycam from May 1, 2023: a reported retail theft, a cart of merchandise, and when Illinois law makes shoplifting a felony.

Share Share on X Facebook Reddit

The most-watched case on The Chest Mount over the last month isn't a chase or a standoff. It's a reported retail theft outside a store in Orland Park, Illinois, where officers stop a man, a cart of merchandise goes back inside, and store staff and police spend most of the video counting items and comparing notes. Below is a neutral walkthrough of what the full Orland Park bodycam footage shows, followed by what Illinois law says about when shoplifting becomes a felony.

Everything here is described as it appears on camera, and quotes come from the audio. The man in the video is accused, not convicted. He is presumed innocent, and nothing in this post should be read as a finding of guilt. We also don't name him here, even though his name is spoken on camera.

The setup: a reported retail theft call

The video opens with an on-screen caption saying that on May 1, 2023, officers in Orland Park "move in quickly" to make an arrest on a reported shoplifting. That caption is the channel's summary of the call. It's not a court finding, and the footage that follows is the only primary record we have.

The first moments move fast. Over the radio and on scene, officers say they have the man, then guide him to sit and "tuck your legs in" as he's placed in a squad car. Before that, an officer asks whether he has anything illegal on him or anything that will "poke me," a standard question before a pat-down. The officer then asks which car is his. The man answers, "I didn't come in a car."

Loss prevention confirms the identification

A couple of minutes in, an officer asks where store loss prevention is, and an employee steps out. Officers ask, "That's him, right?" The employee answers, "Yes," and an officer asks someone to say "positive" on the radio, meaning the identification is confirmed.

That moment matters legally. Illinois lets a merchant who has reasonable grounds to believe someone committed retail theft detain the person for a reasonable time, ask for identification and hand them over to police, under 720 ILCS 5/16-26. Here, officers have already stopped the man, so the store's role on camera is mostly identifying him and documenting the merchandise.

"He changed up his pattern"

One of the most-discussed parts of this case is what staff tell officers about the man's alleged routine. It's also the subject of our Short Store Staff Knew Where His Car Parks?. According to the footage, an employee says, "Usually he comes around 6:00 p.m. and his getaway vehicle parks like right here, but we saw no vehicle. He took longer in the store."

A few minutes later, an officer passes that account along: the man "always parks in the front with a black car" and "usually comes at 6:00 at night," so "he's completely changed up what he normally does." The same officer also says, "she might have been guessing that. I don't know. I haven't really talked to her." That's a useful reminder for viewers. A description of someone's routine on bodycam is a secondhand allegation at that point. Officers say they'll check the store cameras.

Counting the merchandise

Most of the remaining video takes place inside the store as staff and officers inventory what was in the cart. Officers ask for a photo of everything, video of the process and itemized receipts. An officer explains they want photos first and receipts after, and later notes that some of the items appear to be from the store's protected (security-tagged) merchandise.

The exact total is never clearly read out on camera. Partway through the count, someone says "three grand." Later, an employee explains she added up the register subtotals without applying sale discounts, "since we don't do savings for like the theft." The video's title refers to about $4,000. We can't confirm the final number from the footage alone. Retail value is something prosecutors would have to prove (more on that below).

"Three cases today"

Near the end, an officer asks whether the store has an open case involving the same man. Staff say a previous incident was "like 400 approximately," and mention that he had also come in "on Friday." The officer says that if staff have the video and receipts ready, "we'll charge them with three cases today."

That's the officer describing how he plans to write the case, and the footage doesn't show any charging documents. The man also says he has no ID on him, and officers confirm his identity through their system. The Officers Were Already Waiting Short shows the opening moments of the stop.

When is retail theft a felony in Illinois?

"Retail theft over $300 Illinois" is one of the most common searches about shoplifting law in the state, and this case shows why. Under Illinois' retail theft statute, 720 ILCS 5/16-25, retail theft includes knowingly taking merchandise from a store intending to keep it without paying full retail value. The penalties depend mostly on value and prior record:

  • $300 or less (first offense): generally a Class A misdemeanor (the threshold is $150 for motor fuel).
  • $300 or less with certain prior convictions: a Class 4 felony if the person was previously convicted of theft, robbery, burglary, forgery or other listed offenses.
  • More than $300: a Class 3 felony. This applies to a single transaction, or to separate thefts by the same person that are "part of a continuing course of conduct" from one or more stores over one year.
  • Emergency exit: leaving through a designated emergency exit to commit the theft raises the penalty a level.

The "continuing course of conduct" language is why prior incidents, like the roughly $400 incident staff mention on camera, can matter in how a case gets charged. The statute also says that when the charge depends on the value exceeding $300, value is an element of the offense decided by the judge or jury, so the store's receipts and count have to hold up in court.

For context on sentencing, 730 ILCS 5/5-4.5-40 sets a Class 3 felony prison range of 2 to 5 years, and also allows probation of up to 30 months. A Class A misdemeanor carries less than one year under 730 ILCS 5/5-4.5-55. What happens in a real case depends on the facts, the person's record and the court.

Concealment and the "last register" rule

The statute also includes a permissive inference. If someone conceals unpurchased merchandise and carries it past the last place to pay, a judge or jury may infer the person intended to keep it without paying. That's an inference the fact-finder can draw, not an automatic conviction, and the defense can still argue intent. The same section also covers using or possessing a device meant to defeat security tags with the intent to steal.

Your rights if you're stopped for shoplifting

This is general information, not legal advice. If store staff or police stop you over a reported theft:

  • Stay calm and don't run or physically resist. A store detention under 16-26 has to be reasonable in manner and length, but resisting can create new problems.
  • You can decline to answer questions about the incident and ask for a lawyer. Our detained vs. arrested explainer covers what changes when police take over.
  • If you're given a trespass warning for the store, take it seriously. Coming back can lead to a separate charge.
  • Write down names, times and where cameras were. Store video and bodycam can both be requested later.

How to request the Orland Park records

Illinois' Law Enforcement Officer-Worn Body Camera Act (50 ILCS 706/10-20) says recordings flagged for an arrest or detention are disclosed under the Illinois Freedom of Information Act, with people not involved in the encounter redacted. To request reports or footage from a call like this one, send a FOIA request to the Village of Orland Park with the date (May 1, 2023), the location and the type of call. Frequently asked questions

Where and when did this happen?

According to the video's opening caption, officers in Orland Park, Illinois, responded to a reported retail theft on May 1, 2023. The footage does not name the store.

Was the man convicted of retail theft?

The footage does not show any court outcome. On camera, an officer says the case will be charged, but a charge is only an accusation. The man is presumed innocent unless and until proven guilty in a court of law.

When is retail theft a felony in Illinois?

Under 720 ILCS 5/16-25(f), retail theft of property worth more than $300 (or $150 for motor fuel) is a Class 3 felony. That includes separate thefts by the same person that are part of a continuing course of conduct over one year. At $300 or less, a first offense is generally a Class A misdemeanor, but it can be a Class 4 felony for someone with certain prior theft-related convictions. This is general information, not legal advice.

What is the penalty for felony retail theft in Illinois?

Under 730 ILCS 5/5-4.5-40, a Class 3 felony carries a prison range of 2 to 5 years, with probation of up to 30 months also possible. Higher values or theft through an emergency exit can raise the class. A Class A misdemeanor carries less than one year in jail. Actual sentences depend on the facts and the person's record.

Can store employees detain you in Illinois?

Yes, within limits. Under 720 ILCS 5/16-26, a merchant with reasonable grounds to believe someone committed retail theft may detain the person in a reasonable manner and for a reasonable time to ask for and verify identification, ask about unpurchased merchandise, and hand the person over to police. Off the premises, that detention is allowed only during immediate pursuit.
